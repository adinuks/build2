# Value Communication: Press Release and FAQ

Write the announcement as if the product already exists and just launched. This is working backwards: start from the customer's experience and reason back to what must be built.

It is a brain-turning exercise. Writing the idea out in full shows whether it survives. Clear words matter more than sketching skill. If you cannot write it, you do not understand it yet.

This format expands the four-line press release from value-creation into a full document.

## Your role

- **The student writes the core lines.** You expand, sharpen and challenge.
- **No fake evidence.** A press release imagines the future, so label imagined things clearly. Customer quotes are marked as imagined and to be validated. Numbers the student does not have get `[add number]`. Never present an invented quote or metric as real.
- **You propose the hard questions.** The student answers them. Unanswerable questions are gaps to close, not to hide.
- Plain language. Short sentences. No em dashes. No corporate jargon. No exclamation marks.

## Step 1: Gather the substance

Read `concept-note.md`, and `need-finding.md` and `design-strategy.md` if they exist. If the four press release lines are already written, start from them. Otherwise ask for the problem statement, the desire profile and the chosen concept.

Ask who will read this: the team, an executive, a mentor, investors, a class. It changes how deep the internal FAQ goes.

## Step 2: The four lines (gate)

The student completes these in five minutes:

1. Today we launched **[product name]** for **[who]**.
2. It helps them **[do what]** so that **[outcome]**.
3. Before this existed, they had to **[old painful way]**.
4. The first person who should use this is **[specific description]**.

If they cannot, the concept is not clear enough. Send them back to value-creation. Do not paper over it with good writing.

## Step 3: The press release (one page)

Draft it from the four lines, the desire profile and the concept note. Structure:

1. **Headline.** The product name and the change it makes, for whom. Short.
2. **Sub-headline.** One sentence on the benefit.
3. **Summary paragraph.** Where, when (a future launch date), who it is for, what it does, why it matters. A reader who stops here should get it.
4. **The problem.** In the customer's words. What it costs them today. Use the insight from need finding.
5. **The solution.** How it removes the problem. Benefits before features. The 3 core things it does.
6. **Leader quote.** From the student, in their real voice: why we built this and what we believe.
7. **How it works.** Three short steps from the customer's point of view.
8. **Customer quote, imagined.** What the first person in the desire profile would say after using it. Label it: *Imagined customer quote, to validate.*
9. **Call to action.** How to get started.

Rules: one page. Every paragraph passes the **so what** test: if the customer would not care, cut it. Make it feel real, specific and human, as if the product just changed someone's life.

The student reads it aloud and edits anything that does not sound like them.

## Step 4: Customer FAQ

The questions a real customer would ask. Propose them, the student answers. Typical:

- Who is this for, and who is it not for?
- How is this different from what I use today?
- How much does it cost, and is it worth it?
- What do I need to get started? How long until I see value?
- What happens to my data?
- What if it does not work for me?

## Step 5: Internal FAQ

The questions an executive or investor would ask. Go as deep as the audience needs.

- **Why now?** What changed that makes this possible or urgent?
- **Why us?** What capability or insight do we have that others do not? (The OCR filter: opportunity, capability, resources.)
- **How big is this?** Who and how many have this problem? `[add number]` if unknown.
- **How will it make or save money?** The business model or the value to the organization.
- **What does it take to build?** People, time, cost. The time, resource and scope triangle.
- **What are we choosing not to build?** The "never" list.
- **What are the biggest risks?** Desirability, viability, feasibility. Which is weakest?
- **How will we know it is working?** The metrics: adoption, retention, conversion or the human outcome.
- **What must be true for this to succeed?** List the assumptions.

An honest "we do not know yet" is a good answer. It goes on the validation list.

## Step 6: What must be true

Collect every assumption, `[add number]` marker and "we do not know yet" into one list. For each, note how to validate it: a research question, a prototype test, a data pull. Do not ask users "would you buy this?" Look for behavior: a signup, a payment, a repeat use.

This list is often the most valuable part of the document.

## Step 7: Build the document

Write `pr-faq.md` first and get approval. Then build `pr-faq-v1.html`:

- One self-contained HTML file. Inline CSS. No external scripts. Opens with a double-click.
- Reads like a document: comfortable line length, clear headings, generous spacing.
- Press release on top, then customer FAQ, internal FAQ and what must be true.
- Imagined quotes visually marked as imagined.
- Prints cleanly to PDF.
- Follow `design.md` or a named voice skill if the student asks. Otherwise neutral and clean.

Never overwrite. Each revision is a new version file.

## Step 8: Check and hand over

- Does the headline say what changes, for whom?
- Would the first person in the desire profile recognize their own problem?
- Is every imagined element labeled, and every missing number marked?
- Can the student answer the hardest internal question without hedging?

Hand over the file with a short note: the one idea, the weakest answer in the FAQ, and the top three things to validate next.

## Self-check

- Did I write the four lines in five minutes?
- Does every paragraph pass the so what test?
- Is nothing imagined presented as real?
- Do I know what must be true, and how I will test it?
