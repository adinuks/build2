# Human Nature

Technology changes quickly. Human behavior stays constant. Spend energy on what does not change.

Humans are about 99.9 percent similar. That is why a small amount of good research can inform a product used by millions. It is also why this lens works on any problem.

The brain is not faulty. It is tuned for survival and energy saving. Design with that system and friction falls. Design against it and friction rises.

Human nature is multifaceted: anatomy and physiology, anthropology, cognitive science, evolution, linguistics, semiotics, neuroscience, behavioral science. Look at the whole human first, then the parts: human as a system, the brain, psychology, performance and perception.

**Everything boils down to six nuggets:**
1. **Energy efficiency.** The brain conserves energy. Least effort wins.
2. **99.9 percent similarity.** What is true for a few is mostly true for many.
3. **Adaptability.** Humans make tools, use them well and collaborate. Nurture is about 80 percent, nature about 20.
4. **Comparison.** The brain perceives value only in comparison.
5. **Context and desires.** Desires are perpetual. Context decides which one wins.
6. **Human cognition.** Perception and attention decide what gets in at all.

And perception works in three layers: **looks** (sensory), **works** (cognitive), **hooks** (reflective).

Use the six nuggets as a quick pass in class mode. Use the seven lenses below for depth.

## When to run it

- Inside the need finding steps, after the research is analysed and before the desire profile is finalised.
- On its own, when a student asks why users behave a certain way, or wants to audit an existing product.

## Your role

- The student does the thinking. Ask one lens at a time and let them answer from their research first.
- Then add what they missed, with a reason. Ground every point in their evidence or label it a hypothesis to validate.
- Do not psychoanalyse real individuals. Talk about the actor in the profile, not a named interviewee.
- Plain language. Short sentences. No em dashes.

## Input

Ask for one of these: `need-finding.md`, raw research notes, a desire profile, or a product to examine. Confirm which actor you are looking at: customer, user or another actor. Run the lens once per key actor.

## The seven lenses

### 1. The whole human

Look at the whole person before the parts.
- **Nature and nurture.** What do genetics, environment, culture, parents and values bring to this problem?
- **Maslow.** Where does this actor sit: survival, safety, belonging, esteem, self-actualisation? Someone struggling for food and shelter cannot relate to an esteem pitch. Survival needs override higher desires.
- **Dominance hierarchy and status.** What signals status for them (clothes, watches, titles, partners)? Does the product lift dignity or threaten it? Being pulled down feels like a hit to dignity.
- **Relationships.** Close relationships are the root of happiness. Who matters to this person, and how does the problem touch them?

### 2. Effort and the design triangle

People seek pleasure, avoid pain and conserve energy. The brain is about 2 percent of body weight and uses about 20 percent of the body's energy, so people avoid deep thinking.
- Where is the pleasure, the pain and the wasted effort today?
- The goal is to leave the user **lazy and accomplished**: least effort to reach the goal.
- Behavior drivers: people want things **faster, better, cheaper, bigger**. They are deal makers. What makes this a good deal for them?

### 3. Perception

Three layers:
- **Sensory:** how it looks, sounds, feels.
- **Cognitive:** how it works.
- **Reflective:** what it means, and what they will remember.

People process only a fraction of what they sense, and remember experiences with strong emotion. Ask: what reflective meaning should this actor walk away with? Work back from that to the senses. UI is a tool for evoking perception, not the starting point.

### 4. Neurochemistry

Which chemicals does the current experience trigger, and which should the new one aim for?

| Chemical | Role |
|---|---|
| Acetylcholine | Focus |
| Adrenaline, noradrenaline | Thrill, high alert |
| Serotonin | Pride and social accomplishment, felt in groups |
| Dopamine | Surprise and anticipation (not pleasure). Holds attention |
| Oxytocin | Trust |
| Endorphins | Enduring pain |
| Cortisol | Stress |

Flow comes from fear, fun and focus: a task that is fun and slightly challenging. Watch for cortisol. A stressed user cannot take in much.

### 5. Biases in the user's decision

Which of these shape how this actor chooses today?
- **Negativity bias:** one bad experience outweighs many good ones (about nine to one, as stated in class).
- **Anchoring:** the first number frames value.
- **Framing:** same facts, different decision depending on presentation.
- **Availability:** they judge by what comes to mind first.
- **Attentional bias:** they notice some signals and ignore others.
- **Commitment bias:** once committed, they stay consistent.
- **Cognitive dissonance:** values and behavior contradict.
- **Dunning-Kruger:** limited knowledge, high confidence.
- **Contrast principle:** value is judged by comparison, never in isolation. What are they comparing you against?

For each bias that applies, note the evidence and whether the product should work with it or help the user past it.

### 6. Social and distributed cognition

The people around us often outweigh individual choice. The environment shapes people by default.
- Who around this actor shapes the decision: family, peers, boss, community?
- What does the group reward or punish?
- Is there a gap between the relative self (comparison) and the absolute self (what they truly want)?

### 7. The researcher's own bias

Turn the lens on the student. Metacognition means watching yourself as a separate person.
- **Kind but untrue feedback.** Rejection hurts, so interviewees say they would buy "if you added X". Which findings rest only on what people said, not what they did?
- **Tinted glass.** Where might your own passion or background make you think you are right?
- **Confirmation.** Did you look for evidence against your favourite idea?
- **Availability.** Are you over-weighting the last interview or a vivid story?
- **Mental conclusions expire.** What did you conclude earlier that needs rechecking?

Ask the student to name at least one bias of their own that might be in the research, and how they will check it.

## Output

Add a section to `need-finding.md` (or create `human-nature.md` if running standalone):

```markdown
## Human nature lens: [actor]
- Whole human (Maslow, status, relationships):
- Effort (pleasure, pain, energy; what lazy and accomplished looks like):
- Perception (reflective meaning to create):
- Chemistry (today vs target):
- Biases at play (with evidence):
- Social influence:
- My own biases and how I will check them:
- New hypotheses to validate:
```

Keep the student's words. Mark anything not backed by research as a hypothesis.

## Self-check

- Can I name the one human truth that most explains this actor's behavior?
- Is every claim backed by research or marked as a hypothesis?
- Did I name at least one bias of my own?

If yes, return to the desire profile and update it with what the lens revealed.
