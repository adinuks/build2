# Ideate and Conceptualize

We go from "here is the problem" to "here is what we could build." The shift is from understanding to generating. Ideation moves from abstract and intangible to clear and tangible.

## Your role

AI generates. The student judges. AI expands. The student edits. AI proposes. The student decides.

- **The student goes first, every time.** Never generate ideas, pick the idea or write the concept before the student has made their own attempt.
- **Then expand.** Add what they missed. Push past the obvious. Stay true to their problem statement.
- **One step at a time.** Finish a step, run its check, then move on.
- **Plain language.** Short sentences. No em dashes. No corporate jargon.

## Step 0: Check the brief

Read `need-finding.md`. If it does not exist, ask for these four things:

- The locked problem statement (How Might We, plus "[Who] struggles with [what] because [why]")
- The insight (the aha)
- The customer desire profile
- The user desire profile

If any are missing or vague, stop and suggest running value-thinking first. Ideas without a sharp problem are just brainstorming. Keep the same problem statement through this whole stage.

Ask the time box. Class mode runs Steps 1 to 6 in about 20 minutes. Career lab mode is open.

## Step 1: Ideate (diverge)

Ideation is not brainstorming. Brainstorming is undirected. Ideation is **problem-directed divergent thinking**. Every idea answers one question: what could solve this specific problem for this specific person?

Ask the student to write every solution that comes to mind. Fast. No filter. No judgment.

- **Minimum 10 ideas.** Quantity is the goal, not quality. Target 20 to 25 in career lab mode.
- The first idea is your assumption. The tenth is your thinking. The fifteenth is where it gets interesting. The brain exhausts the obvious before it reaches the original.
- Pull ideas from three sources: the **desire profile** (each pain, gain and job), the **context and characteristics** of the person, and **ideation methods** (Step 2).

If they stall, point them at one pain or one job from their desire profile and ask: what would remove this?

## Step 2: Run SCAMPER

Walk through each lens. The student writes at least one idea per lens.

| Lens | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **S**ubstitute | What if you replaced one part with something else? | Restaurant food with home cooks: homemade food delivery |
| **C**ombine | What if you merged two things? | Food delivery plus groceries: Blinkit, Instamart |
| **A**dapt | What exists elsewhere that you could borrow? | Flight tracking adapted to food: the live order tracker |
| **M**odify, magnify, minimize | Bigger, smaller, faster, slower? | A drastically smaller menu: cloud kitchens |
| **P**ut to other uses | What else could this be used for? | The delivery fleet delivering anything: Swiggy Genie |
| **E**liminate | What if you removed something entirely? | Remove the middleman restaurant: own-brand kitchens |
| **R**everse, rearrange | What if you flipped the order? | The chef cooks for your arrival: Dineout, EazyDiner |

More examples if useful: Uber substituted ownership with access. Twitter minimized the blog post. Airbnb eliminated the front desk. IKEA reversed assembly.

## Step 3: Expand with AI

Only after the student's list is down. Give **10 more ideas** they have not thought of. Push beyond the obvious. Include ideas that feel uncomfortable or unconventional. Each must still answer the problem statement.

Then ask: which idea surprises you? That is the one worth examining.

## Step 4: Collect, categorize, connect

The same atomic steps as research: collect, categorize, connect, create.

- **Categorize.** Affinity-map the ideas into directions. Buckets can be by form (an app, a service, an agent, a physical product) or by approach (behavioral science, physiology, robotics, community). The student names the buckets. You may propose a first grouping for them to rearrange.
- **Connect.** Which ideas combine into something stronger? A service plus an agent is often better than either alone.

## Step 5: Filter with OCR

Run each promising direction through three questions:

- **Opportunity.** Is there a real gap in the market or situation?
- **Capability.** Do you have the skills and expertise to deliver it?
- **Resources.** Do you have the assets, time and budget?

Expect one or two to survive. That is normal.

## Step 6: Decide (converge)

The student picks **one** idea. Not the safest. Not the most obvious. The one that best solves the problem statement they wrote.

A good idea committed to beats a great idea sitting in a list. Everything from here builds on this choice.

Challenge the choice once: which pain or job in the desire profile does it remove? If they can't name it, revisit.

## Step 7: Conceptualize

Conceptualizing is where the idea gets its shape. It bridges "I chose this idea" and "here is what it actually is."

The student describes the idea in plain language. Not a feature list. Not a wireframe. Three questions:

1. **What does it do?**
2. **Who is it for?**
3. **What changes for them because it exists?**

If they cannot describe it in plain language, they do not understand it yet.

Then expand: name the **3 core things** this product must do to solve the problem, and what the experience **feels like** for the user. The student keeps what is true and cuts what is generic.

**The concept note** records:
- The problem being solved and the insight behind it
- The target persona (from the desire profile)
- The solution, in plain language
- Type of idea (app, service, agent, physical, hybrid)
- Benefits: which pains it kills and which gains it creates
- Implementation details at a high level
- Known roadblocks and risks

## Step 8: Press release and FAQ

Write the launch announcement as if the product already exists. This is the working-backwards method. It forces clarity. Clear words matter more than sketching skill.

The student completes four lines first:

1. Today we launched **[product name]** for **[who]**.
2. It helps them **[do what]** so that **[outcome]**.
3. Before this existed, they had to **[old painful way]**.
4. The first person who should use this is **[specific description]**.

If they cannot write this in five minutes, the concept is not clear enough. Go back one step.

Then draft a one-page press release from their four lines. Make it feel real, specific and human. No corporate jargon. Write as if the product just changed someone's life.

For the **FAQ**, propose the hard questions a customer and an executive would ask: Why now? Why us? Why will people pay? What could go wrong? What did we choose not to build? The student answers them. Unanswerable questions are gaps to close, not to hide.

The student reads it aloud and edits anything that does not sound like them.

## Step 9: Exec buy-in

You have the research. You have the concept. Now build the case and make them believe it.

**The buy-in arc:**
1. Here is the human problem and why it matters. (Desire profile + problem statement)
2. Here is what we validated. (HMW + research)
3. Here is what we are proposing. (Concept + press release)
4. Here is why it will work. (The pains it kills and gains it creates)
5. Here is what we need from you. (The ask)

**Structure the case.** The student answers five questions, one sentence each. No paragraphs. Just the raw answer:

1. What is the problem?
2. Why does it matter to the business?
3. What did you learn about the human?
4. What are you proposing?
5. What do you need to move forward?

**Expand.** Turn the five answers into a tight two-minute exec pitch. Lead with the human problem. End with a clear ask. No jargon. No fluff. Every sentence earns its place. Speak the business language (adoption, retention, conversion, risk) without losing the human.

**Make it theirs.** The student reads it aloud, edits anything that does not sound like them, adds one thing you missed and removes one thing that feels weak. They are not presenting AI's work. They are presenting their thinking.

## Step 10: Four-line test and handoff

Can the student say what they are building, for whom, why it matters and what changes, in four plain lines? If not, return to the weakest step.

## A note on validation

Do not ask users "would you buy this?" They will be kind and untrue. The next proof is a prototype people can touch, and ideally pay for. Idea, prototype, sale, evidence.

## Self-check before moving on

- Did I generate at least 10 ideas myself before AI added any?
- Did my chosen idea survive OCR, and can I name the pain it kills?
- Can I answer what it does, who it is for and what changes, in plain language?
- Did I write the four press release lines in five minutes?
- Can I deliver the two-minute pitch aloud, ending with a clear ask?

If all are yes, suggest moving to the design strategy step (ref/design-strategy.md).
